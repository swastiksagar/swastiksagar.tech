How this works

Most SOC material teaches in scattered pieces. A tool walkthrough here, a certification cram-guide there, a "day in the life" video somewhere else. The pieces rarely line up. You can pass a multiple-choice exam and still freeze the first time a real alert lands in your queue, because knowing a term and knowing what to do with it are different skills.

This curriculum is the spine. Three tiers, ninety-six lessons, one career track — networking and log fundamentals at the bottom, detection engineering and threat hunting at the top. Every lesson opens into three parts: the explanation, a plain-language "why it matters on shift" line, and a breakdown — every specific term, port, event ID, or item the lesson mentions, spelled out one by one instead of buried in a paragraph.

Beyond the curriculum: a roadmap for how to move through it over time, a cheatsheet of the commands and references you'll reach for constantly, and five playbooks for the incident types that show up again and again.

Contents

Tap a tier to expand its modules. Tap any lesson title to read it — you'll get a full explanation, a "why it matters" line, and a breakdown of every term it mentions. The curriculum is split into three tiers that map directly to how real SOCs are staffed: Tier 1 is triage and fundamentals, Tier 2 is investigation and response, Tier 3 is hunting and detection engineering. Work top to bottom — each tier assumes you've absorbed the one before it. Nothing here is gated: there's no signup, no paywall, and no "premium" track. If a lesson references a tool you don't have yet, the Stack section further down lists every one of them, and the Cheatsheet holds the exact syntax so you're never stuck guessing.

How each lesson is built: every lesson has the same three parts — a plain explanation of the concept, a "why it matters" line that ties it to real SOC work, and a term-by-term breakdown table so unfamiliar vocabulary never blocks you. Any breakdown term shown in a dark chip is a real command, filter, or path — click copy next to it to grab the exact text and paste it straight into your own terminal, SIEM search bar, or homelab.

What "Learn", "Lab", and "Build" actually mean

Every lesson in the curriculum below carries one of three colored tags. They aren't difficulty levels and they aren't in a fixed order within a module — they describe the kind of work the lesson asks of you, so you know what you're walking into before you open it and can plan a study session around the mode you're in the mood for.

Learn

Pure conceptual grounding. A Learn lesson builds the mental model — what a concept is, why it exists, and how it fits into the bigger picture — before you're asked to touch a single tool. There's no software required beyond reading; the job here is absorbing vocabulary and frameworks you'll lean on in every Lab and Build lesson that follows. These are the lessons to do first in a new module, and the ones worth re-reading if a later Lab feels confusing.

Examples in this curriculum
  • Ports, protocols & services
  • Windows processes, services & the registry
  • Malware families: trojans, ransomware, worms, RATs
  • Severity & priority scoring

Lab

Hands-on practice inside a real tool, log source, or dataset. A Lab lesson puts you inside Wireshark, a SIEM search bar, an email header, or a sandbox report and has you apply the concept from the matching Learn lesson to something concrete. This is where recognition turns into reflex — you're not just told what a DNS tunneling pattern looks like, you're shown the actual log line and asked to spot it. Labs are the closest thing on this page to sitting at a real analyst desk.

Examples in this curriculum
  • DNS: records, resolution, tunneling abuse
  • Windows Event Logs: Security, Sysmon, PowerShell
  • Reading email headers & SPF/DKIM/DMARC
  • URL & attachment triage with sandboxes

Build

Produce something you keep. A Build lesson has you create an artifact — a note, a report, a rule, a script, a calculation — the kind of output you'd actually hand to a teammate or drop into a portfolio. This is deliberate: reading about an escalation note and writing one yourself are completely different skills, and hiring managers care about the second. Anything you make in a Build lesson is fair game to reference later in interviews or in the Portfolio & Job Search module.

Examples in this curriculum
  • Subnetting & CIDR by hand
  • Writing an escalation note
  • Sigma rules: write once, deploy anywhere
  • Writing a malware analysis report that survives peer review

Use the Show filter just below to view only one type at a time — for example, filter to Lab alone if you've got an hour and just want reps, or to Build alone if you're assembling portfolio pieces for a resume push. Most study sessions work best moving through a module in the order it's listed: Learn to load the concept, Lab to see it in real data, Build to prove you can produce something with it.

Roadmap

A realistic self-paced timeline from zero to Tier 3-ready. Adjust the pace to your own hours available — the order matters more than the calendar.

New to all this? Start at Stage 1 no matter your background — nobody skips it. "Tier 1 / 2 / 3" is just SOC-speak for junior → mid → senior analyst work, and it lines up with the tiers in the Contents section above. Each stage below tells you plainly what you'll actually be doing, then the specifics. Any underlined term has a plain-English definition — just hover or tap it.
1
MONTHS 0 – 3
Foundations
Tier 1
In plain terms: learn how networks and computers actually work before touching a single security tool.
  • Networking fundamentals — how packets actually move, common protocols (TCP/IP, DNS, HTTP), and basic subnetting.
  • Windows and Linux internals — processes, file systems, permissions, and where each OS keeps its logs.
  • Core security concepts — the CIA triad, authentication vs. authorization, and the common attack types you'll see referenced everywhere else.
  • Your first real reps reading a SIEM — just getting comfortable navigating logs and dashboards.
  • Don't skip the OS internals lessons to rush toward "real security" content — nearly every triage decision later depends on this layer.
Milestones for this stage
  • Begin studying for Security+
  • Stand up a basic Wazuh instance in a homelab
2
MONTHS 3 – 6
First Tier 1 reps
Tier 1 → 2
In plain terms: start doing the job for real — even an internship — while you keep learning. Real tickets teach faster than any lab.
  • Start applying for Tier 1 / SOC Analyst I roles or internships — don't wait until you feel fully "ready."
  • Finish the back half of Tier 1 while you're working or interviewing, not before.
  • Start Tier 2's SIEM deep-dive and network security monitoring modules in parallel.
  • Real ticket volume, even at an MSSP, will teach pattern recognition faster than any lab ever could.
Milestones for this stage
  • Pass Security+
  • Land a Tier 1 role or internship
  • Publish your first homelab write-up
3
MONTHS 6 – 9
Investigation depth
Tier 2
In plain terms: stop just flagging alerts and start fully investigating and closing them yourself, start to finish.
  • EDR tools — how to pivot from a SIEM alert into endpoint-level detail.
  • Threat intel basics — using indicators of compromise and threat feeds to add context to an alert.
  • The incident response lifecycle — identification, containment, eradication, recovery, and the after-action report.
  • Digital forensics fundamentals — what evidence to collect and how to preserve it properly.
  • Cloud monitoring — reading AWS/Azure logs and understanding cloud-specific attack patterns.
  • Applied malware analysis — enough static and dynamic analysis to describe what a sample actually does.
Milestones for this stage
  • Pass CySA+ or SC-200
  • Handle a full incident from detection to closure
4
MONTHS 9 – 14
Hunting & detection engineering
Tier 3
In plain terms: instead of waiting for alerts to fire, you start writing the rules that catch attackers before they do damage.
  • Threat hunting — proactively searching for what your existing alerts are missing, instead of waiting for them to fire.
  • Sigma/YARA rule writing — building your own detections instead of relying only on vendor defaults.
  • Purple teaming — working alongside a red team exercise to see which detections held up and which didn't.
  • Enough reverse engineering to go past a sandbox verdict and explain why a sample is malicious.
  • This is the tier that shifts you from "closes tickets well" to "improves the SOC's actual detection coverage."
Milestones for this stage
  • Pass GCIH
  • Publish real Sigma/YARA rules with write-ups
  • Run your first purple team exercise
5
MONTHS 14+
Specialize & grow
Tier 3+
In plain terms: pick a lane — forensics, threat intel, or leadership — and go deep instead of broad.
  • Deeper forensics and incident response path — working toward GCFA.
  • Deeper threat intel and reverse engineering path — working toward GCTI/GREM.
  • Governance and SOC leadership path — moving from doing the work to shaping how the team does it.
  • Use the Career track to turn everything you've built into a resume, a portfolio, and a Tier 2/3 offer.
Milestones for this stage
  • Promoted to Tier 2/3, or hired directly into it
  • Specialty certification earned

Three homelabs worth building

Reading about a SIEM and running one are different skills. These are the labs that actually differentiate a SOC resume.

LAB 01

Wazuh SIEM & XDR

Stand up Wazuh, feed it real endpoint and network telemetry, write custom decoders and rules, and detonate contained malware samples to prove your alerts actually fire.

LAB 02

Splunk at scale

Ingest tens of thousands of real DNS and auth events, build SPL searches that surface beaconing and credential abuse, and ship dashboards a Tier 2 analyst would use.

LAB 03

Nessus vulnerability program

Run authenticated scans across a lab network, triage findings by real exploitability instead of raw CVSS, and produce a remediation report a system owner could act on.

About this project

SOC Analyst from Scratch is a free, open curriculum that writes out every core topic a Security Operations Center analyst needs — from OSI layers to detection engineering — instead of pointing at scattered blog posts and hoping they add up.

Why it exists

You can pass a certification exam and still not know what to do when a real alert lands, because reciting a definition and making a triage decision under time pressure are different skills. This curriculum is written the way a senior analyst would actually explain something to someone new on the floor: what it is, why it matters here, and exactly what each piece of vocabulary means.

How it is made

Every lesson follows the same shape: a short read, a plain-language "why it matters" line, and a term-by-term breakdown — grounded in the tools and log sources a working SOC actually uses. Corrections and additions are welcome; this is meant to be forked, edited, and run on your own machine, not treated as gospel from a vendor.

Who it's for

Anyone studying for a Tier 1 SOC role, anyone already on the floor who wants the Tier 2/3 path mapped out, and anyone building a homelab who wants to know which lab actually proves something to a hiring manager.
